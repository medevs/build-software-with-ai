    The highest-leverage move you'll make, and it isn't writing code

    Evolving the System

    When the same mistake keeps happening, the fix isn't a better fix. It's a better context layer.

    The mistake that keeps coming back

    You've probably felt this already: the agent makes a mistake, you correct it, and next session it makes the same mistake again. Maybe it keeps using the wrong naming pattern, or skips a validation step you always want run, or reaches for a library your team stopped using. Each time, you patch it in the moment — and each time, the underlying cause is still sitting there, waiting to bite the next session too.

    That's the signal to pay attention to: not "the agent got this wrong," but "the agent got this wrong again." A single mistake is just a mistake. A repeated mistake is telling you something about your system.

    Fix the system, not the bug

    The key move When a mistake recurs, stop building for a moment and have a conversation about what to change in your context layer — usually your rules — so that whole class of failure gets less likely, not just this one instance of it.

    This is a different move than fixing the bug in front of you. Fixing the bug gets today's code correct. Fixing the system means the next ten times that situation comes up, the agent is less likely to trip on it at all — because you wrote the lesson down somewhere it'll actually see it again.

    In practice this usually means sharpening a rule: adding the convention you keep having to restate, or tightening a constraint that was too vague to actually prevent the mistake. Sometimes it means adjusting a skill's steps, or adding a new guardrail. The exact fix varies, but the target is always the same — the context layer, not the one file you're editing right now.

    Why this compounds

    The context layer is the one thing about your setup that you fully own and carry forward. Every time you fold a lesson back into it, every future work session starts from a slightly better baseline — not just for you, but for anyone else working in the same project.

    Hand-fix only

    You fix today's instance. Tomorrow, the same class of mistake is exactly as likely as it was yesterday. You're spending effort but the baseline never moves.

    Fix the system

    You fix today's instance and update the context layer. Tomorrow, that class of mistake is rarer. The baseline moves up, and it stays up.

    Nothing about your starter context layer is meant to be final. Rules, skills, and the rest are a beginning, not a finished product — the expectation is that you'll keep shaping them as you learn where your project's real trouble spots are.

    Eventually, build the thing that builds the thing

    Once you've written a handful of rules by hand, or a handful of skills, something becomes obvious: writing them is itself a repeated process. So encode that. A skill whose job is to interview you and write a new skill. A specialist whose job is to author and retune other specialists. You've stopped improving the work and started improving the thing that improves the work.

    The order matters, and it isn't optional: understand it, build a few by hand, then build the thing that builds the thing. You can only bake in preferences you actually hold, and preferences come from having done it yourself a few times. A meta-tool built on a shallow understanding just automates your mistakes faster. But get there and the payoff compounds — every later addition to your system costs less than the last, because more of the authoring is delegated.

    Don't overcorrect This isn't a call to stop and rewrite your rules after every tiny slip. Most of the time you keep building — the loop doesn't need to run perfectly to keep moving. Reach for a system fix specifically when something goes wrong more than once, or when it's clearly a big deal even the first time.

    Don't wait for the mistake

    Waiting for a mistake to repeat works, but it's reactive — it only ever fixes what has already cost you something. As your system grows, two more deliberate motions take over.

    Find the gap

    Read how you actually work — the corrections you keep typing, the checks you keep running by hand — and let that tell you what to encode next. If your agent keeps a readable history of your sessions, "read my last month of sessions and tell me what I repeat" is a genuinely useful prompt.

    Guard the gap

    Check that the piece you added is actually good — and stays good after you edit it, or after the model underneath it changes. An improvement you never check is an improvement you're taking on faith.

    The first half has one trap worth naming: it surfaces patterns, it doesn't decide for you. Noticing that you retype the same three sentences every session is easy; deciding whether that becomes a rule, a skill, or a guardrail is your call, and the six building blocks are the menu you pick from.

    For the second half, "did it work?" is the obvious question. Two less obvious ones are worth asking every time you change your setup:

    What did it cost?

    Time and tokens. A workflow that gets there through twenty tool calls when five would do is a workflow worth trimming.

    What did it improvise?

    What did the agent invent on the fly because your rule or skill didn't cover it? Today's improvisation is tomorrow's permanent step.

    Additions aren't automatically wins It's tempting to assume every rule and skill you add makes the agent better. It doesn't work that way — an addition that helps on most tasks can still make a handful of them worse, by pulling the agent toward a workflow that didn't fit them. That's the argument for checking instead of assuming.

    Checking doesn't have to be elaborate. Keep three or four tasks you care about — a commit message, a small refactor, a review — and re-run them by hand after any meaningful edit to your rules or skills. Left alone, a context layer quietly stops matching how you want to work: you edit it, the code moves, the model under it changes. You won't notice that by feel. You notice it because something you decided mattered is getting checked.

    One convenient thing about both motions: they're the same question pointed at different evidence. Aim it at a single run — the notes, the plan, the review it produced — and you get the reactive version: this went wrong here, what should change so it can't recur. Aim it at weeks of your own history and you get the proactive version: nothing is broken, what keeps costing me anyway. Same question, different pointer. Which is also why the artifacts of a run are the natural target — they're already scoped to that run, so there's no archaeology to do.

    The way this actually dies You ask the question, you get a good report, you nod, and you change nothing. Your agent will always find something to improve — so the report is never the deliverable. A report you didn't act on is worse than no report, because it costs time and buys the feeling of progress. Pick one item and apply it. One change applied beats ten recommendations read.

    Two loops, one habit

    This is the same "outer loop" idea from earlier in the course, now made concrete: most of your time is spent in the inner loop, actually building. But every so often — a recurring problem, or a pattern you spotted in your own history — is your cue to step out of that loop briefly, patch the context layer, and step back in with a slightly better system than you had before.

    Check yourself

    The agent makes the exact same kind of mistake for the third time this month. What should that trigger?

    Why doesn't just hand-fixing each recurrence of a mistake make things better over time?

    Recalling the two loops from earlier in the course: which loop is "evolving the system" an example of?

    Beyond waiting for a mistake to repeat, what are the two more deliberate ways to evolve your system?

    The one thing to remember

    When a mistake recurs, fix the context layer, not just the code — and check the changes you make to it instead of assuming they helped.
